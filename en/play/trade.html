Trade Ship Tycoon

Load Korean exports onto your ships and sell them abroad. Purchase prices, freight and sale prices all come from real monthly customs data, and you find out which year it was at the end.

  1. Pick Easy or Hard. Hard also sets your starting money (from $0), starting ships (0–3) and length. The start month is random, and you find out which it was at the end.
  2. On Hard, first buy or charter ships at the ship market above. Types differ in size, price, upkeep, freight and accident risk, and buying (ship finance) and chartering (deposit, three-month minimum) come with different terms. Raise money with bank and policy loans, and on each ship card choose trade finance (leverage), insurance and a forward contract.
  3. Each month, choose a cargo, a destination and a cargo value for every ship. Each card previews containers plus freight and fees (Hard adds weight and purchase price).
  4. You sail at the start of the month, so this month's figures are not out yet. 'Market' shows how last month's prices and exports by country changed from the month before, plus this month's freight. Use them to guess the month ahead. In Hard, upkeep, charter hire and loan interest go out every month, so you need to earn more than the 'Fixed costs this month' shown above the cargo cards.
  5. Hit 'Set sail'. A month later each ship is settled: purchase, freight, fees, sale and profit.
  6. On Hard you can also pay for clues about next month at the information market. Pricier tips are more detailed and more reliable, but false tips slip in. You can skip them and trust your gut.
  7. At the end you see your net worth, the real period, and how a single product and destination would have done.
  8. Mid-game, 'End business' shows your results so far, 'Restart' starts over and 'Change difficulty' takes you back to Easy/Hard. Progress saves every month, so 'Continue' picks up where you left off.
Export price per kg
What Korea earned exporting that product that month, divided by its weight. It sets both your buying and selling prices.
Freight
The cost of shipping goods. The game uses the cost of sending one 40-foot container.
40-foot container
A steel box about 12 m long. Here it holds up to 20 tonnes, and a ship carries up to 50.
Freight and fees share
Freight plus port and insurance costs, divided by the cargo value. The sale price includes a 3% base margin, so above 3% you lose money unless prices rise over the month.
Port fees
The cost of loading, unloading and storing goods at the port.
Cargo insurance
Cover for goods damaged at sea. On Hard, uninsured ships can have accidents.
Demand
Whether Korean exports to that country rose or fell from the month before. Rising demand lifts your sale price.
Base margin
The markup a trader adds when prices hold steady. It's 3% here.
Upkeep
Crew, fuel and repairs you pay just for owning a ship, even an idle one.
Loan interest
Interest charged monthly on what you owe. 0.6% a month is about 7.4% a year.
Chartering
Renting a ship instead of buying one. No big upfront cost, but you pay hire every month, and hire rises with freight.
Trade finance
A bank loan that pays for the goods you export. You repay it from the sale.
Leverage
Adding borrowed money so you trade more than you own. At 2x, gains and losses on your own money double.
Policy loan
Cheap government-backed credit for exporters. Here it's low-interest but can't be repaid early.
Emergency loan
Expensive short-term money borrowed when you run short. It costs 1.5% a month here.
Forward contract
A deal that fixes today the price you'll sell at later. You're safe if prices fall but miss out if they rise.
Ship finance
A loan secured on the ship you buy. Selling the ship repays it first.
Deposit
Money you leave with the owner when you charter a ship. Keep the ship for the agreed time and you get it back.
Feeder ship
A small container ship that shuttles between big hub ports and smaller ones.
Net worth
Cash plus the book value of owned ships and deposits, minus everything you owe.
  • Purchase price: that month's export value per kg (Korea Customs exports ÷ export weight)
  • Freight: that month's sea freight on the route (customs-declared rate, one 40-foot container, treated as US dollars); one container per 20 tonnes, up to 50 per ship
  • Port fees: 0.5% of the purchase. Cargo insurance: 0.5% (always on in Easy)
  • Sale price: next month's price per kg × (1 + 3% base margin) × (1 + half of the change in Korean exports to that country, capped at ±20%)
  • Hard starts with the money you pick ($0, $100,000, $500,000, $1,000,000 or $3,000,000) and 0–3 debt-free mid-size ships. There are four ship types. Small feeder: 15 containers, buy $60,000, upkeep $4,000 a month, 10% more freight. Mid-size container ship: 50, $150,000, $10,000. Large container ship: 120, $380,000, $22,000, 15% less freight. Used mid-size ship: 50, $90,000, $14,000, 5% more freight and 2.5x the accident risk. An owned ship's book value (its price) falls 1% a month, and selling brings 85% of book value.
  • You can buy with ship finance, borrowing 70% of the price at 0.5% a month; selling the ship repays it first. Chartering needs no purchase: pay a deposit of one month's hire, then hire every month (upkeep included). Hire is a base rate (feeder $8,000, mid-size $20,000, large $45,000, used $14,000) × the freight index in the month you sign (each route's freight relative to its own average, averaged), fixed for the contract. The minimum is three months; return earlier and you lose the deposit.
  • Bank loans come in $100,000 steps up to half your net worth at 0.6% a month and can be repaid any time. With zero net worth the limit is zero, so with no collateral or track record you start with the policy loan or ship finance. The policy loan is a one-off $300,000 at 0.3% a month, cheaper but locked until the end. If you run short, an emergency loan at 1.5% a month kicks in automatically.
  • Trade finance (leverage): for each ship the bank can fund half (2x) or two thirds (3x) of the cargo. The borrowed part costs 0.7% for the month at sea and is repaid from the sale first. Gains on your own money are two or three times bigger, and so are losses.
  • Forward contract: when you sail, the sale price is fixed at this month's price per kg plus the 3% base margin, for a fee of 1% of the cargo value. Whatever prices and demand do next month, you sell at that price.
  • Information market (Hard): a low-grade tip costs $2,000 for one rumor (right 60% of the time), mid-grade $8,000 for two industry reports (75%), high-grade $25,000 for three research notes (90%). Tips come from next month's real data (export price per kg, Korean exports by country), and a false tip points the wrong way. Low-grade tips cover anything; mid and high grades pick what is about to move most. Each grade can be bought once a month, and after settlement you see which tips were right, with the actual figures.
  • An uninsured ship has a 3% chance (7.5% for a used ship) of an accident that wipes out 60% of the sale. Net worth below −$200,000 means bankruptcy. 'End business' at the top shows your results so far at any time. On Hard, ending early costs an early repayment fee (2% of bank loans and ship finance; the policy loan is exempt) and a wind-down cost (one month's upkeep per owned ship, for crew severance and lay-up), and chartered ships under three months forfeit their deposits.
  • Easy is scored by return; Hard by what you earn (final net worth − starting net worth). Net worth is cash + book value of owned ships − ship finance + deposits − other loans. The US uses the US West Coast route; Germany uses the Europe route. The margin, demand weight and accident odds are game settings. Ship prices and hire are scaled far below real life (a real container ship costs tens to hundreds of millions of dollars and charters for tens of thousands a day) to match cargo values set by real prices. Real shipping lines also typically borrow 50–70% of a ship's price through ship finance.
