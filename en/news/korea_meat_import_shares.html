Korea meat imports: Brazil chicken, Australian beef, Spanish pork gain share

Over the past 12 months Brazil supplied 80% of Korea's chicken-cut imports by value, up from 71%, while Thailand fell to 16%. Australia gained in frozen beef even though US beef became duty-free in January, and Spain overtook the US in frozen pork import value despite a higher average price per kilogram. Our calculations from Korea Customs Service data.

Written by AINov 17, 2026
Report an error

01Chicken: Brazil's share climbs to 80%

Brazil supplied 79.8% of the $618 million of chicken cuts (HS 020714) South Korea imported in the 12 months to October 2026, according to our calculations from Korea Customs Service data. A year earlier, in the 12 months to October 2025, its share was 71.1%. Thailand's share fell from 24.7% to 15.6%. These shares are of imports only, not of total consumption including domestic meat.

The earlier 12 months include events from spring 2025. In May that year a highly pathogenic bird flu outbreak on a Brazilian poultry farm led Seoul to halt imports of Brazilian chicken, and the agriculture ministry moved to let in meat from disease-free areas of Brazil, a process known as regionalization. The suspension fell inside the earlier 12-month period, and total imports of this line rose 20.1% by weight. Brazil's volume rose 27.8%, from 158,000 tonnes to 202,000, while Thailand's fell 18.9%, from 39,000 tonnes to 32,000.

Brazil's price per kilogram rose 18.5%, from $2.06 to $2.44, yet it was still about 20% cheaper than Thai chicken at $3.04. Thailand shipped $104 million of cooked chicken under another code, more than the $97 million in this line, so this line alone does not show its whole position in Korea.

02Duty-free US beef loses ground

Korea's tariff on US beef fell to zero on Jan. 1, 2026, the last step of the phase-out under the Korea-US Free Trade Agreement. Yet the US share of Korea's frozen boneless beef imports (HS 020230) slipped from 36.4% to 33.5% by value, while Australia's rose from 57.3% to 62.2%. Australian volume grew 9.2%, from 143,000 tonnes to 157,000; US volume edged up 1.5%, from 79,000 tonnes to 80,000.

Australian beef got pricier too: $7.64 a kilogram, up 17.7% from $6.49, against $8.02 for US beef, up 7.4% from $7.46. The US premium narrowed from 15% to 5%.

Australia still faces a tariff and a volume cap. Under its own trade agreement with Korea, Australian beef paid 5.3% in 2026, according to the Australian livestock outlet Beef Central and the food-market publication Foodmarket, but the safeguard volume of about 196,000 tonnes was used up in July, triggering a 24% rate for the rest of the year. US beef, duty-free all year, already held a tariff edge; from late July the gap widened to 24 percentage points. The latest 12 months still include Australian beef that cleared before the cap was reached. Declared customs values exclude duty, so the tariff gap does not show up in the per-kilogram prices. The in-quota rate drops to 2.6% in January 2027. In chilled beef (HS 020130), the US held 70.2%, little changed from 69.3%.

03Pork: Spain gains with a higher average price

Frozen pork (HS 020329) tells a different story. Spanish volume jumped 62.6%, from 98,000 tonnes to 159,000, lifting its share of import value from 22.3% to 28.1%. US volume fell 2.7%, from 175,000 tonnes to 170,000, and its share dropped from 31.8% to 26.9%. Total frozen pork imports rose 18.7% by weight.

Spain was not the cheaper supplier. Its average fell 10%, from $3.93 a kilogram to $3.54, but that was still 12% above the US average of $3.17. This code lumps together cuts from belly to shoulder and ribs, so the gap may reflect a different mix of cuts rather than a higher price for the same product. Disease controls may also have played a part. After African swine fever was found in wild boar near Barcelona in late November 2025, Japan suspended all imports of Spanish pork, while Korea blocked only meat from the affected area, according to Korean press reports. Some Spanish supply may have shifted to Korea as a result, but the trade data cannot confirm it.

04What meat suppliers should watch

In none of the three lines does price alone explain the shift in share. Chicken follows a ban and its lifting; in beef, the US tariff cut did not stop Australia gaining; in pork, the gap may reflect a different mix of cuts. Before benchmarking a per-kilogram price against a rival, check whether the same HS code is hiding different cuts or levels of processing.
