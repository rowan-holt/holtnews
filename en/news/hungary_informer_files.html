Hungary makes public the names of 60,000 communist-era secret police informers

Thirty-seven years after communism fell, anyone can now search the names.

HOLT NEWS
Nov 5, 2026

01What happened

On November 4, Hungary put its communist-era secret police files into an online database. Anyone can now look up about 60,000 names of informers and agents.

Until now, only researchers, the people named in the files, and their close relatives could ask to see them. Detailed files on about 9,000 people will follow in February.

02Who were the informers?

Before communism fell in 1989, secret police across Eastern Europe recruited ordinary citizens as helpers, often through threats or rewards.

These informers reported on friends, neighbors, coworkers, and sometimes family. One survivor of a forced labor camp said people would learn that there were traitors in every family and on every street.

03Why now?

After the change of system, one government after another put off fully opening the files. Many say the political will was missing.

Prime Minister Peter Magyar, who won the April election, promised to open them. He said Hungary is finally keeping a promise made at the end of communism. Polls showed more than two-thirds of Hungarians supported opening the files.

04Other countries

Most former communist countries opened their files earlier. Files of East Germany's secret police, the Stasi, became open in 1992, soon after German reunification. The Czech Republic opened its files in 2003 and Latvia in 2018.

05What remains unclear

The files are far from complete. Many were burned during the 1956 uprising against Soviet rule and as the regime fell in 1988 and 1989. Some estimate that records survive for only about a third of roughly 150,000 informers.

Appearing on the list does not prove that someone chose to cooperate. Officials and historians stress this point. Sensitive details such as health, addiction and sexuality, along with information about victims, will be blacked out.

Magyar asked people to treat the files "with dignity and humanity." A historian warned that the release must not turn into public shaming. The day before the release, former prime minister Viktor Orbán said the secret police had tried to recruit him during military service and that he refused.

06What it could mean

Many families may now face the past of parents or neighbors who have died. Debate is likely to continue between those who say wounds cannot heal while the past stays hidden and those who fear people forced by threats will be branded for life.

When the detailed files come out in February, it will become clearer who reported on whom. Checks on the past of officials and public figures may also begin in earnest.
