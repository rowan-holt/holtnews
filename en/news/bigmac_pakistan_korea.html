The Big Mac index 2026 puts Pakistan and South Korea at almost the same price

In The Economist's July 2026 Big Mac index, a Big Mac cost $3.89 in Pakistan and $3.84 in South Korea. That is almost the same price. Measured against income, the burden is more than 20 times heavier in Pakistan.

Written by AINov 22, 2026
Report an error

In brief

  • In The Economist's July 2026 Big Mac index, a Big Mac cost 1,080 rupees ($3.89) in Pakistan and 5,700 won ($3.84) in South Korea.
  • One Big Mac equals 84% of a day's GDP per person in Pakistan and 3.9% in South Korea.
  • On The Economist's GDP-adjusted index, the won is 32% undervalued against the dollar and the Pakistani rupee 19%.

01Five cents apart

A Big Mac in Pakistan now costs five cents more than one in South Korea. In The Economist's Big Mac index for July 2026, the burger sold for 1,080 rupees in Pakistan, or $3.89. In South Korea it was 5,700 won, or $3.84.

Of the 54 places in the index, Pakistan ranks 41st by dollar price and South Korea 42nd. A Big Mac in the US costs $6.22, so both countries are close to 40% cheaper than America. Five cents is, for practical purposes, the same price.

02What the index measures

The index rests on a simple idea called purchasing power parity: the same item should cost the same everywhere once you convert the currency. If 5,700 won buys the same burger as $6.22, a dollar should be worth about 916 won.

The exchange rate The Economist used in July was 1,485.9 won to the dollar. By the burger test, the won trades 38.3% below where it should. Run the same sum for Pakistan and a dollar should buy about 174 rupees. It actually bought 277.9, which makes the rupee 37.5% undervalued.

So near-equal dollar prices tell you the two currencies look about equally cheap against the dollar. They say nothing about whether life costs the same in Pakistan and South Korea.

A weak won cuts both ways. Visitors spending dollars find Korean prices low, and exporters paid in dollars receive more won. Koreans travelling abroad, and firms that buy raw materials in dollars, pay more. The index itself does not measure these effects.

03Same price, a very different burden

To see the burden, compare the price with income. The Economist's dataset includes GDP per person estimated by the IMF: $1,695.6 for Pakistan and $36,227 for South Korea. Divided by 365, that is $4.65 a day against $99.25.

One Big Mac equals 84% of a day's GDP per person in Pakistan and 3.9% in South Korea, a gap of more than 20 times. The source file lists euro-area countries one by one, so 71 countries have GDP data. Among them, Pakistan has the highest share. India comes in at 33% and the US at 2.5%.

04In 2021, Korea's Big Mac cost 67 cents more

In January 2021 a Big Mac in Pakistan cost 550 rupees and a dollar bought 160.35 rupees. By July 2026 the dollar bought 73% more rupees, and the burger's price had risen 96% to 1,080 rupees. In dollars, it went from $3.43 to $3.89.

Over the same period South Korea's price rose 27%, from 4,500 won to 5,700 won, while the dollar bought 35% more won, 1,485.9 instead of 1,097.35. Because the burger price lagged the currency, its dollar price fell from $4.10 to $3.84.

In Pakistan the burger's price rose faster than the dollar's exchange rate. In Korea it rose more slowly. That is how a burger that cost 67 cents more in Korea in January 2021 now costs five cents more in Pakistan. The data do not show how either market sets its prices.

05Adjusting for income

Burgers tend to be cheaper in poorer countries, partly because rent and wages, which do not cross borders, cost less. The Economist publishes a second version of the index that allows for this. It takes the IMF's GDP per person, re-adjusts it by the gap in Big Mac prices, estimates what a burger should cost at that level, and compares that with the real price. So the GDP figures behind it differ from the ones above.

On that measure a Korean Big Mac should cost about $5.68. At $3.84, the won is 32% undervalued. Pakistan's expected price is $4.81, so the rupee is 19% undervalued. Adjusted for income, the won looks even cheaper than the rupee.

06What to keep in mind

The Big Mac index is not a cost-of-living index. It uses one burger to sense-check exchange rates, and that price also reflects who McDonald's targets in each market, local taxes and competition.

GDP per person is not a salary. It is national output divided by population, so in countries with uneven incomes it can sit far from what an ordinary worker earns.

In recent years The Economist has published new figures dated January and July. The January 2027 release will show which way the won and the rupee have moved by the burger test.
