What is a chaebol? Samsung, Hyundai and Korea's family-run business groups explained

Korea's antitrust regulator lists 102 business groups with at least 5 trillion won (about $3.7 billion) in assets, led by Samsung. In the 89 that have a controlling person, usually a family, the family directly owns 3.5% of member companies' shares on a capital-weighted average. Counting the group's own companies, insiders hold 61.4%.

Written by AINov 24, 2026
Report an error

In brief

  • The Korea Fair Trade Commission, the country's antitrust regulator, designated 102 business groups with 3,538 member companies as of May 1, 2026, each with at least 5 trillion won in assets; Samsung ranked first with 695.8 trillion won in assets.
  • In the 89 groups that have a controlling person, the family directly owned a capital-weighted average of 3.5% of member companies' shares in 2026, affiliates owned 55.5% and all insiders together 61.4%, the commission's ownership report shows.
  • A July 2025 amendment to Korea's Commercial Act requires directors to act for both the company and its shareholders, and from Sept. 10, 2026, large listed companies can no longer use their articles of incorporation to block cumulative voting for directors.

01The short answer

A chaebol is a group of Korean companies controlled by one person or family through shareholdings that run from company to company. Samsung, SK, Hyundai Motor and LG are the four biggest. The Samsung phone, the Hyundai SUV and the LG fridge in an American home each come from a different company, but each of those companies sits inside a group of many others.

The Korea Fair Trade Commission, the country's antitrust regulator, tracks them as large business groups. Each group has a "same person" (dongilin in Korean), the individual or company the commission treats as controlling it and as ultimately responsible for following the rules for large groups. For Samsung that is Lee Jae-yong, for SK Chey Tae-won, for Hyundai Motor Chung Eui-sun and for LG Koo Kwang-mo. The commission's list is a regulatory category, not a legal definition of chaebol. Of this year's 102 groups, 13 have no controlling person; some are run by a company or by a cooperative federation.

02How many, and how big

Every spring the commission names the groups big enough to face extra rules. As of May 1, 2026, it designated 102 groups with at least 5 trillion won in assets, about $3.7 billion at 1,343.6 won to the dollar, the rate on Nov. 23. They include 3,538 companies. All of them must disclose large deals between their own companies and may not give unfair benefits to the controlling family. The 47 largest, each with at least 12 trillion won (about $8.9 billion), an amount set at 0.5% of Korea's 2,408.7 trillion won GDP, face more. Their companies may not hold shares in each other or create new circular shareholdings, guarantee each other's debts, or freely vote affiliates' shares held by their financial and insurance arms.

Samsung tops the list with 67 companies and 695.8 trillion won in assets on the commission's measure, about $518 billion. That is 18.1% more than a year earlier, a period in which the group also went from 63 companies to 67. SK follows with 422.0 trillion won, Hyundai Motor with 320.8 trillion and LG with 186.3 trillion. We added up the assets of all 102 groups: the top four hold 44.3% of them.

03Control with 3.5% of the shares

How can a family run companies it barely owns? The commission's September report on the 89 groups that have a controlling person shows the numbers. Across their Korean member companies, weighted by capital, the family directly owns 3.5% of the shares on average. The group's own companies own 55.5%. Add shares held by group nonprofits and executives, and shares the companies have bought back from the market, and insiders hold 61.4%. The average hides a wide range. At Iljin Global, the controlling person himself owns 51.4% of the group's shares, the highest of any group.

Picture the average as 1,000 shares. The family holds 35. Affiliates hold 555, and other insiders about 24, a figure we got by subtraction. That leaves 386 with outside shareholders. In some groups the family owns a large stake in a company near the top, often a holding company that exists mainly to own shares in others, and that company owns the next ones down. The commission says the gap between direct family ownership and insider holdings across the group remains substantial.

Circular shareholdings, in which company A owns B, B owns C and C owns A, fell across the 102 groups from 1,435 loops in 2025 to 233 in 2026. More than the whole net drop came from one group, Sajo, which cut its loops from 1,426 to 220. Sales between a group's own companies were large. In 2025, 35.0% of the revenue of 2,871 Korean member companies, 841 trillion won or about $626 billion, came from selling to affiliates: 11.5% to affiliates in Korea and 23.5% to affiliates abroad. The commission says such deals can cut transaction costs, but can also be used to hand unfair gains to particular affiliates.

04Where Americans run into them

Most Americans meet a chaebol as a shopper. Some meet one as a worker. Samsung announced an estimated $17 billion chip plant in Taylor, Texas, in November 2021, its largest US investment at the time. SK hynix, part of the SK group, agreed in April 2024 to invest an estimated $3.87 billion in a chip packaging plant in West Lafayette, Indiana, with more than 1,000 jobs.

Investors in Coupang, the e-commerce company listed in New York, meet the rules too. Coupang is among the 102 designated groups. Until this year the commission listed the company itself, not a person, as the group's same person. On April 29 it named founder Bom Kim instead, effective May 1. It found that his brother held a vice-president-level role at Coupang and had chaired hundreds of meetings on delivery policy, so Coupang no longer met the legal conditions for a company to be named. The commission said it wanted the same person to match whoever actually controls the group.

05What changed in 2025 and 2026

For investors, the open question has long been whose interest a chaebol board serves. Korea's Commercial Act now answers part of it. An amendment promulgated in July 2025 says directors must act for the company and its shareholders and treat all shareholders fairly. Before, the law named only the company.

A second amendment, promulgated on Sept. 9, 2025, took effect on Sept. 10, 2026. Listed companies above an asset threshold set by decree can no longer write cumulative voting out of their articles of incorporation. Shareholders with at least 1% of the voting shares must still ask for it in writing six weeks before the meeting. When they do, a shareholder voting in an election for three directors gets three votes per share and can put them all on one candidate, which makes it easier for minority holders to seat a director. The same law raised the number of audit committee members elected separately from the other directors from one to at least two at listed companies that must have an audit committee.

The cumulative-voting rule applies from the first shareholders' meeting called to elect directors after Sept. 10, 2026. The commission's next data release, on how group boards are run, is due in December.
