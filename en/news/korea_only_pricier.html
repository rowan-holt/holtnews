Dominant firms charging Koreans more than buyers abroad could face penalties under a proposed competition rule

Dominant firms that charge more in Korea without good reason could be found to be abusing their power.

Written by AIPublished Nov 7, 2026 · Updated Nov 10, 2026
Report an error

01What happened

On November 6, Korea's Fair Trade Commission announced a draft change to the rules under its competition law. It changes how the agency judges abuse by companies that dominate a market. This is the first change to that standard since 2001.

The draft is open for comments until December 16. After review by other ministries and the government's legislation office, the commission plans to finish the change in the first half of 2027.

02Why now

The law already bans dominant firms from unfairly setting, keeping or changing prices. But the detailed rules only covered price changes.

That made it hard to act when a firm set Korean prices higher than abroad from the start, or kept prices high after costs fell. Even for price rises, the agency had to prove they were "markedly" larger than changes in supply, demand or cost. The commission says that bar was so hard to meet that the rule was barely used.

03What changes for prices

The draft lets the agency judge how prices are set and kept, not only how they change. It also lowers the bar from "markedly" to "substantially."

Whether a price is unfair depends on two checks: is it substantially above cost, and is it also substantially above the usual price of the same product in other markets, or of similar products? If both are true and there is no good reason, it could count as abuse. The commission named unfairly charging more in Korea than abroad as a leading example.

Keeping prices high is covered too: raising prices quickly when costs rise, but not lowering them when costs fall.

04Beyond prices

Tying becomes its own type of abuse: forcing buyers of one product to also deal with the firm, or a firm it picks, for another product or service.

Using a partner's private business information, gained through deals, to compete against that partner is added. So is blocking partners from dealing with consumers directly or sharing information with them.

Rules on blocking rivals also widen. They used to focus on manufacturing, such as cutting off raw materials. Now they cover blocking the purchase, access, use or movement of any goods or services, tangible or intangible, that a business needs, so they can apply to every industry, not only manufacturing.

The draft also spells out what harming consumers means: unreasonable terms, cutting quality in ways that clearly hurt consumers, unfairly limiting choice, and imposing serious disadvantages.

05Are penalties automatic?

No. The commission must first decide whether a company dominates its market, looking at market share, how hard it is for new firms to enter, and the size of competitors. Under the law, a firm is presumed dominant if it alone holds at least 50% of a market, or if three or fewer firms together hold at least 75%.

Then it checks whether there is a good reason for the higher price in Korea.

06What it could mean

If the draft is adopted, consumers could gain grounds to challenge subscription fees and service prices that are higher than abroad. Dominant firms could be asked to explain why Korean prices differ.

There is no set number yet for what "substantially" means. The real impact depends on how it is read and whether a first case is brought. The wording could become more specific during the comment period. The first test will be which case the commission takes up after the change is finished in 2027.

Corrections

  • Nov 10, 2026 The headline now makes clear the rule targets dominant firms and is still a draft. The price test now follows the draft text (the same product in other markets, or similar products), 'access' was added to the business-obstruction rule, and wording not in the source ('digital services', firms 'would need data') was changed to 'every industry' and 'could be asked to explain', and consumers 'would gain' grounds became 'could gain' them if the draft is adopted.
