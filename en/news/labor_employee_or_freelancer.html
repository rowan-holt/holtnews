South Korea's fake freelancers: when a contractor is owed severance

In South Korea, a contract that calls you a freelancer does not settle whether you are one. If the company directed your work, the courts can treat you as an employee, with severance pay and the other money that comes with it.

Written by AINov 15, 2026
Report an error

01Who the law covers

South Korea's main employment law, the Labor Standards Act, and a separate law on severance pay protect employees, and only employees. So before anyone can claim severance pay, a paid weekly rest day or coverage under Korea's four social insurance schemesSocial insurance is defined in Korean law as a system that protects people's health and income by dealing with social risks through insurance.Framework Act on Social Security, Art. 3(ii)Chapter 21 Social insurance deductions → (pension, health, employment and workplace-injury insurance), there is a prior question: does the law see you as an employee at all?

The Labor Standards Act defines an employee as anyone who works for a business in return for wages, whatever the job. The title on the contract does not decide it. A deal labeled a freelance, service or commission agreement still creates an employee if the work looks like employment. The reverse can also hold: someone with an employment contract who in practice runs their own business may not be an employee.

The biggest sum riding on that answer is usually severance. Korea's Employee Retirement Benefit Security Act requires employerUnder Korea's Labor Standards Act: the business owner, a person managing the business, or anyone else acting for the owner on matters concerning employees.Labor Standards Act, Art. 2(1)(ii)s to pay it to anyone who has worked for at least a year, averaging at least 15 hours a week measured over four weeks. It applies to businesses of any size. The only exceptions are businesses that employ nobody but relatives living with the owner, and domestic workers hired by a household. Some other entitlements, such as annual paid leave and overtime premiums, depend on how many people a business employs.

This chapter reflects the Labor Standards Act as it stands from October 8, 2026, and the Employee Retirement Benefit Security Act as it stands from September 18, 2026.

02How the rules changed

The test for who counts as an employee comes mostly from Supreme Court rulings rather than the text of the law, and it has shifted over time.

In a December 1994 ruling, the court said what matters is the reality of the work, not the form of the contract. But it asked whether the employer gave specific, individual instructions and supervision.

In December 2006, it spoke instead of substantial direction and supervision, a change widely read as lowering the bar. It also said courts should not easily deny employee status just because there was no fixed base pay, no income tax withheld from wages, or no enrollment in social insurance as an employee. Those, it reasoned, are things an employer can arrange as it likes.

From December 1, 2010, severance rules were extended to businesses with four or fewer employees, with at least half the normal amount owed for work up to the end of 2012 and the full amount from 2013.

In July 2024, it said that for people who find work through an app, judges should apply the same factors with the realities of platform work in mind, such as an algorithm deciding who gets which job.

So the common belief that a 3.3% tax deduction makes you a freelancer with no severance does not match the test courts have applied since 2006.

03The problem: fake freelancers

Koreans call the practice "fake 3.3." A business hires people who work like employees but signs them up as self-employed contractors. Instead of withholding income tax from wages, it deducts a flat 3.3% (3% national tax on business income plus 0.3% local tax), the rate applied to payments to the self-employed. The business avoids social insurance contributions, severance and various allowances, and the worker loses all of them.

Using withholding-tax records from the National Tax Service and tips from labor groups, the Ministry of Employment and Labor, South Korea's labor department, picked 108 suspect workplaces and inspected them between December 1, 2025, and March 5, 2026. At 72 of them, or 67%, it found 1,070 employees signed up this way. Across all the workplaces inspected, 1,126 workers had been denied paid weekly rest days or annual leave, or had not received overtime, night or holiday pay, with unpaid wages totaling 685 million won, about $514,000.

The first case the ministry announced, in January 2026, was a popular restaurant business with six branches in Seoul that had registered 38 of its 52 workers, 73%, as self-employed. It owed 51 million won, about $38,000, to 65 current and former staff.

After the inspections ended, the ministry said on July 1 that all the workers found without coverage had been enrolled retroactively in employment and industrial accident insurance, and about 520 million won ($390,000) in unpaid premiums had been collected from the businesses.

Those were the cases inspectors found. Elsewhere, the burden falls on the worker: under current law, the person claiming to be an employee has to prove it. The contract and tax records say whatever the company wrote, so the worker has to show what the job was really like.

04Where views differ

The first dispute is over what should count most. Korean courts put the weight on how far a company directs and supervises the work. Critics say they should look harder at how far a person depends on one company for a living. The argument has sharpened as platform work has grown, where an app gives the instructions and the jobs come from a single source.

The second is over who should carry the burden of proof. In January 2026, the labor ministry said it would introduce a presumption of employment: once someone shows they personally did work for another's business, they would be treated as an employee unless the business proves otherwise. The change would be made by amending the Labor Standards Act, and the ministry has said it aims to get it through the National Assembly by December 2026.

Supporters say the company, which holds the records, should carry the burden. One delivery rider, they point out, needed four and a half years from losing the job to being recognized in court as an employee. Opponents say a business would struggle to prove a negative, that there was no direction or supervision, so disputes would multiply, small firms would bear the cost and companies might hand out less work. As of November 15, 2026, the amendment had not passed.

05What the courts decided

Korea's Supreme Court weighs a set of factors together. Does the company decide what the work is and supervise it substantially? Does it set the hours and the place? Can the worker use their own equipment or hire someone else to do the job? Do they carry the risk of profit and loss themselves? Is the pay a return for labor? And how long, and how exclusively, have they worked for the company?

The 2006 case involved instructors at a hagwon, a private cram school, preparing students for university entrance exams. Each year they signed what was called a lecture services contract. They were registered as businesses for tax purposes, had business income tax withheld and carried health insurance as individuals rather than through an employer. The court still found they were employees. The school set their arrival times, teaching hours and classrooms, and paid them an hourly rate multiplied by hours taught, so their pay did not change with the number of students.

The 2024 case involved a driver for Tada, an app-based service that rented out vans with a driver attached. The driver had signed a "freelance driver" contract with a staffing agency and was dropped from the roster by a group chat message. In this unfair-dismissal case, the court ruled that the driver was an employee, and that the employer was not the agency but Socar, the car-sharing company that supplied the vans and owned Tada's operator.

Courts also draw the line the other way. In the 1994 case, the plaintiffs were registered businesses that had taken on subcontracted construction work, each directing a crew of roughly 16 to 26 laborers they had hired themselves. The court found they were independent contractors, even though the company supplied equipment or materials, enrolled them in workplace-injury insurance and received daily reports from them.

Case law can change, and the spread of platform work could shift the test again.

06Where that leaves us

Under today's test, neither the contract's label, nor the 3.3% deduction, nor the lack of social insurance settles that someone is not an employee. How tax was withheld is one factor among many. What matters is who decides the work, who fixes the hours and place, and whether the worker bears business risk with their own money.

Courts weigh the factors case by case, so people in the same line of work can get different answers. In a dispute, the evidence that shows the reality is ordinary: clock-in records, messages giving instructions, work rosters and records of pay received.

There are time limits too. Severance must be paid within 14 days of leaving, though both sides can agree to a later date if there is a special reason. Even so, interest of 20% a year runs from the day after that 14-day deadline. The 20% rate may not apply for any period in which it is reasonable for the employer to contest in court or before the Labor Relations Commission whether the money is owed at all, for instance whether the worker was an employee. The right to claim severance expires after three years.

Unpaid severance can be pursued through a complaint to a regional labor office or in court. Being recognized as an employee can also bring some tidying up. Income reported as business income may need to be corrected to wage income for tax purposes, and retroactive enrollment in social insurance can mean the worker owes their share of the premiums.

07Do the math

Once someone is recognized as an employee, severance comes to roughly one month of average payUnder Korea's Labor Standards Act, the average wage is total wages paid in the three months before the event that triggers the calculation, divided by the number of days in that period.Labor Standards Act, Art. 2(1)(vi)Chapter 14 Average wage → for each year worked. Years are counted as days worked divided by 365, so two years and two months earns about 2.17 years' worth.

Take someone paid 2.5 million won (about $1,880) a month with 3.3% withheld, who worked for two years (730 days) and left on September 30. Their severance would be about 4,891,304 won, roughly $3,670.

The calculation uses the 2.5 million won before the deduction, not the 2,417,500 won that reached the worker's account. The 3.3% is tax paid in advance; it does not reduce the wage. Try your own start date, last day of work and monthly pay in the calculator below.

08If you run a business

A contract that says freelancer will not settle the matter if you direct the work and set the hours and the place. Korean courts look at how the work was actually done, not at the label on the contract.

If the worker is found to be an employee, unpaid severance and allowances and back premiums for social insurance can all come due at once. The labor ministry picks workplaces to inspect using withholding-tax records and anonymous tips.

Paying up does not end it. Failing to pay severance or wages within 14 days of someone leaving is a crime punishable by up to five years in prison or a fine of up to 50 million won, about $37,500. Prosecutors cannot proceed if the worker does not want the employer punished, but that exception does not apply while the employer is on the government's published list of wage defaulters.

Not handing over a written employment contract can bring a fine of up to 5 million won. Not issuing pay slips, or not registering a worker for employment insurance, can bring administrative penalties of up to 5 million won and 3 million won respectively.

Late severance can carry interest of 20% a year. And if wages were withheld deliberately, or left unpaid for three or more months in a year, the worker can ask a court for up to three times the amount owed.
