Korean women grew taller than any other women in the world over 100 years

A century of trends in adult human height
NCD Risk Factor Collaboration · eLife, 2016
2026.10.21

01The result

How much taller is a Korean woman born recently than one born a century ago? A network of around 800 researchers from around the world set out to answer questions like this.

Comparing adults born in 1896 with those born in 1996, Korean women grew 20.2 cm taller on average. That is the biggest gain for women among the 200 countries and territories studied.

Korean men grew 15.2 cm over the same period. Among men, the biggest gain was in Iran, at 16.5 cm.

02How they studied it

The researchers gathered 1,472 studies. Altogether, more than 18.6 million people had their height measured.

They left out heights that people simply reported about themselves and used only measured heights, because people tend to add a little when they report their own height.

They then estimated average height at about age 18 for each birth year, tracing the change from people born in 1896 to those born in 1996.

03How the world changed

Among people born in 1996, the tallest men were Dutch, at 182.5 cm on average. The tallest women were from Latvia, at over 168 cm.

At the other end, men in Timor-Leste averaged around 160 cm and women in Guatemala around 149 cm. The gap between the tallest and shortest countries was more than 20 cm.

Not every country kept growing. Countries that grew tall early, like the United States, leveled off and dropped in the rankings. In some African countries, people born after the 1960s were actually shorter than earlier generations.

04Korea and its neighbors

Korea climbed the rankings sharply over the century. Today, Korean men and women are taller on average than their Japanese peers.

But the rise for Korean men began to level off with those born in the 1980s. Height does not keep going up forever.

05Why the change?

This paper did not analyze the causes country by country. Still, height is not set by genes alone. Nutrition, illness, hygiene and living conditions in childhood, and even before birth, are known to play a big part.

That is why a country's average height is sometimes used as a sign of how well it feeds and cares for its children. The big change for Korean women can be read alongside a century of major changes in daily life.

The researchers also note that height is linked to health. Studies suggest taller people have a slightly lower risk of heart disease and a slightly higher risk of some cancers. Height is best seen as a signal of childhood nutrition and health, not a goal in itself.

06Limitations

This study did not follow the same people for 100 years. It compared the height at about age 18 of different generations, born in 1896 and in 1996.

Data from a century ago is far thinner than today's, so heights for older generations are estimates from statistical models and carry some uncertainty.

These are averages. Any one person's height can be very different.
